Ancient Roman temples were designed as physical houses for their deities rather than gathering spaces for worshipers. Most religious rituals took place outside at an altar in front of the building.

Key Architectural Features

• High Podium: Unlike Greek temples that sit on low steps all the way around, a Roman temple stood on a high stone platform (podium).
• Single Front Entry: You could only enter the temple from the front by walking up a single, central staircase.
• Deep Porch (Portico & Pronaos): The front featured a deep colonnaded porch supported by columns. The open area between the front columns and the inner room was called the pronaos.
• The Cella: Behind the porch sat the cella (or naos), a closed inner room that housed the statue of the god or goddess. This space was private and usually only entered by priests.
• Attached Columns: While the front columns stood free, the columns along the sides and back were often half-columns (engaged columns) built directly into the exterior walls to create a sense of symmetry.
• Influences & Materials: Roman temple design blended Etruscan front-facing layouts with Greek classical column styles (Doric, Ionic, and Corinthian), later incorporating advanced Roman concrete and massive domes (such as seen in the Pantheon).

Notable Examples

• The Pantheon: A remarkably preserved temple dedicated to all the gods, famous for its massive unreinforced concrete dome and central oculus (opening) in the ceiling.
• Temple of Portunus: A small, well-preserved rectangular temple located in the Forum Boarium near the Tiber River, featuring an Ionic colonnade and a high podium.
• Temple of Venus and Roma: Commissioned by Emperor Hadrian, this was the largest temple in ancient Rome, dedicated to Venus and the spirit of the city of Rom
