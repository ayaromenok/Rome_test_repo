A text description of the map of the Imperial city of Rome covers key geographical features, walls, hills, and monumental centers.

The Tiber River and Lowlands

• Tiber River: Curves through the city from the north toward the south, serving as the main artery for trade, transport, and water supply, spanned by several bridges.
• Campus Martius: A large, low-lying flat area enclosed by a bend of the Tiber River, used for military exercises, public assemblies, and major monumental building programs.

The Seven Hills

The core of Rome is anchored by its famous hills, which offered natural defense and elevated ground for temples and palaces:
• Palatine Hill: The center of imperial power, home to grand imperial palaces and overlooking the Roman Forum.
• Capitoline Hill: The religious citadel featuring the great Capitoline Temple (Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus).
• Quirinal Hill: Located to the northeast, featuring the Temple of Serapis and the Baths of Constantine.
• Viminal Hill: A smaller central hill used largely for residential quarters and gardens.
• Esquiline Hill: A broad eastern hill containing the sprawling park and palace grounds of Nero’s Domus Aurea and the Baths of Trajan.
• Caelian Hill: Situated to the southeast, characterized by aristocratic villas, residential neighborhoods, and early churches.
• Aventine Hill: Located to the south near the river, housing various temples and viewed as a traditional residential or luxury zone.

Central Public Spaces and Arenas

• Roman Forum (Forum Romanum): The political, legal, and commercial heart of the city, nestled in the valley between the Palatine and Capitoline hills.
• Circus Maximus: A massive long chariot-racing stadium located in the valley between the Palatine and Aventine hills, capable of holding hundreds of thousands of spectators.
• Colosseum (Flavian Amphitheatre): Situated east of the Roman Forum, near the Caelian and Esquiline hills, used for gladiatorial games and public spectacles.

Defensive Walls and Infrastructure

• Aurelian Walls: Built beginning in 271 CE by Emperor Aurelian to encircle the expanded urban area and protect the city.
• Aqueducts: Networked masonry channels that carried fresh water across the valleys and hills to public fountains, bathhouses (thermae), and private villas
